The inaugural question

What does it mean for AI to be native to space?

Space is more than a place to deploy Earth-trained systems. Unfamiliar physical conditions, mission constraints and scarce scientific opportunities can reshape the learning problem itself.

As an emerging independent research initiative, Space-Native AI opens a public research agenda for those working at the intersections of machine learning, aerospace, robotics, life sciences and autonomous discovery.

Our 2026 activity invites short, non-confidential questions and perspectives. No live attendance is required: this is an asynchronous open call, not a scheduled webinar or a formal paper-review conference.

Open research questions

Three problems worth investigating.

01 · Learning & adaptation

Learning under altered physical conditions

How can intelligent systems generalize and remain reliable beyond the conditions represented in Earth-based training data?

Research angles include dynamics shifts, world modeling, simulation-to-reality transfer, continual adaptation and uncertainty calibration.

02 · Autonomous science

Closed-loop scientific discovery

Can an experimental agent decide what to measure, learn from observations and choose its next scientific action in space?

Research angles include active learning, scientific agents, optimal experiment design, robotic laboratories and hypothesis refinement.

03 · Mission-aware learning

Intelligence under severe constraints

How should AI allocate observations and experimental attempts when energy, computation, communications and materials are scarce?

Research angles include constrained decision-making, information gain, data efficiency, reliability and risk-aware planning.

Featured exploratory topic

One Liter in Space

What could an intelligent laboratory discover with a conceptual one-liter payload?

Consider which questions to investigate, what observations matter, how a system could learn from feedback, and how constraints might change the scientific strategy.

The 10 × 10 × 10 cm volume is a thought-experiment boundary, not an offer of flight hardware, launch capacity or a flight-ready CubeSat specification.

How to participate

Open questions, not a paper competition.

Bring a research problem or a speculative concept. Contributions need not include results, code or proprietary methods.

Submission guidance ↗

Choose a direction

Learning in Space, Autonomy in Space, Bio in Space, Living in Space or a cross-disciplinary problem.

Write 100–250 words

Explain the scientific question, why the space setting matters and how one might evaluate progress.

Post publicly via GitHub Issues

Participation is free. A GitHub account is required to post, and submissions are public; do not include confidential content.
